BUGATTI

POWER REFINED TO PERFECTION


What is bugatti?

Bugatti is the embodiment of automotive artistry and engineering excellence. Renowned for crafting hypercars that push the boundaries of speed, luxury, and design, Bugatti stands as a symbol of elite performance and exclusivity. Each model—from the iconic Chiron to the ultra-rare Divo and Bolide—is a fusion of cutting-edge technology and meticulous craftsmanship, built not just to drive, but to captivate. With its unmistakable horseshoe grille and aerodynamic silhouette, Bugatti isn’t just a car—it’s a statement of power, precision, and prestige.

How it all stated?

Bugatti’s story begins in 1909, when Ettore Bugatti, a visionary Italian-born engineer with a deep artistic heritage, founded Automobiles Ettore Bugatti in the town of Molsheim, then part of the German Empire. Born in Milan in 1881 to a family of artists—his father, Carlo Bugatti, was a celebrated furniture designer, and his brother, Rembrandt Bugatti, a renowned sculptor—Ettore inherited not only a passion for aesthetics but also a relentless drive for mechanical innovation. His early career included apprenticeships and collaborations with various automotive firms, but his uncompromising standards and creative independence led him to establish his own marque, where he could fuse engineering precision with artistic elegance. The first Bugatti prototype, the Type 10, was a lightweight, compact car that prioritized agility over brute force, a philosophy that would define the brand’s DNA. This evolved into the Type 13, Bugatti’s first production model, which featured advanced engineering for its time, including an overhead camshaft and four valves per cylinder. The Type 13 made its racing debut at the 1911 French Grand Prix, finishing second and instantly establishing Bugatti as a serious contender in motorsport. World War I disrupted operations, forcing Ettore to relocate to Milan and Paris, but after the war, he returned to Molsheim—now part of France—and resumed production with renewed vigor. The post-war years saw the release of the Type 13 Brescia, named after its racing success in Italy, which became a symbol of Bugatti’s resilience and technical prowess.

The 1920s and 1930s marked the golden era of Bugatti, with the introduction of legendary models like the Type 35, arguably the most successful racing car of all time, boasting over 2,000 victories thanks to its lightweight chassis, advanced suspension, and signature eight-spoke alloy wheels. Bugatti didn’t just dominate the racetrack—it also redefined luxury with models like the Type 41 Royale, a car built for royalty with a massive 12.7-liter engine and unparalleled opulence, and the Type 57 Atlantic, a design masterpiece with flowing lines and riveted seams, now considered one of the most valuable cars in the world. During this period, Ettore’s son, Jean Bugatti, emerged as a brilliant designer and engineer in his own right, refining the Type 57 and introducing innovations such as independent suspension and hydraulic brakes, further elevating Bugatti’s reputation for sophistication and performance. Tragically, Jean Bugatti died in 1939 while testing the Type 57C, a devastating loss that deeply affected Ettore and the company. World War II brought further challenges, including the seizure of the Molsheim factory and a halt in production. After the war, Ettore attempted to revive the brand, but without Jean and facing financial difficulties, Bugatti struggled to regain its former glory. Ettore Bugatti passed away in 1947, and the company released its final model, the Type 101, in the 1950s, based on pre-war designs but unable to compete with modern automotive trends. By 1963, Bugatti ceased automobile production entirely, and the brand lay dormant for decades, surviving only through its legacy and the reverence of collectors and enthusiasts. In the late 1980s, Italian entrepreneur Romano Artioli acquired the rights to Bugatti and attempted a revival, establishing Bugatti Automobili S.p.A. and building a new factory in Campogalliano, Italy. This effort produced the Bugatti EB110, a technologically advanced supercar with a quad-turbocharged V12 engine and all-wheel drive, but despite its innovation, the venture was short-lived and ended in bankruptcy by the mid-1990s.

The true rebirth of Bugatti came in 1998, when the Volkswagen Group acquired the brand and committed to restoring its prestige. Under VW’s stewardship, Bugatti reemerged as a hypercar manufacturer, debuting the Veyron 16.4 in 2005—a groundbreaking vehicle with a quad-turbocharged W16 engine producing 1,001 horsepower and capable of speeds over 400 km/h. The Veyron shattered records and redefined what was possible in automotive engineering, blending extreme performance with luxury and craftsmanship. Bugatti continued to evolve, releasing even more powerful and exclusive variants like the Veyron Super Sport, which held the title of the world’s fastest production car for years. In 2016, Bugatti introduced the Chiron, a successor to the Veyron, featuring an even more refined W16 engine with 1,479 horsepower, advanced aerodynamics, and cutting-edge technology, solidifying Bugatti’s position at the pinnacle of automotive excellence. The Chiron spawned limited-edition models such as the Divo, focused on handling and agility; the Centodieci, a tribute to the EB110; and the Bolide, a track-focused beast designed for maximum performance. Each model exemplified Bugatti’s commitment to pushing boundaries while honoring its rich heritage. Today, Bugatti stands not just as a car manufacturer but as a symbol of artistry, innovation, and elite engineering, with its headquarters still in Molsheim, now a state-of-the-art facility where tradition meets technology. The brand’s journey—from Ettore’s artistic vision and Jean’s engineering brilliance to its modern-day resurrection—reflects a legacy of passion, resilience, and an unwavering pursuit of perfection that continues to inspire awe across generations.

Eye on future:

Bugatti’s future is a bold fusion of heritage and innovation, driven by a relentless pursuit of excellence that transcends mere automotive engineering and ventures into the realm of art, technology, and lifestyle. As the brand transitions from its legendary W16 engine era—capped by masterpieces like the Chiron Super Sport and the open-top W16 Mistral—it enters a new chapter defined by electrification, sustainability, and ultra-luxury experiences. The centerpiece of this evolution is the Bugatti Tourbillon, a next-generation hypercar powered by a naturally aspirated V16 hybrid powertrain, designed to deliver breathtaking performance while embracing the future of mobility. This marks a significant shift in philosophy, blending raw mechanical emotion with cutting-edge electric precision, ensuring that Bugatti remains at the forefront of automotive innovation without compromising its soul. The Tourbillon is not just a car—it’s a statement of intent, showcasing Bugatti’s commitment to craftsmanship, advanced aerodynamics, and intelligent design, with every detail meticulously engineered to honor the brand’s legacy while pushing boundaries. Beyond the cars themselves, Bugatti is expanding into lifestyle domains, launching Bugatti Home with bespoke furniture collections and opening flagship boutiques in global luxury capitals like Dubai, Milan, and Paris. These ventures reflect Ettore Bugatti’s original vision of creating a holistic luxury experience, where engineering and artistry coexist in every facet of life. The brand’s Sur Mesure program continues to offer clients unparalleled personalization, allowing them to co-create vehicles that are as unique as their fingerprints, while Bugatti’s Grand Tour events and exclusive racetrack experiences deepen the emotional connection between car and driver.

Technologically, Bugatti is investing in lightweight materials such as carbon fiber and titanium, AI-enhanced driving systems for adaptive performance, and sustainable production practices that reduce environmental impact without sacrificing quality. The company is also exploring advanced battery technologies and modular platforms that could underpin future electric models, ensuring that Bugatti remains relevant in a rapidly changing automotive landscape. At its core, Bugatti’s future is about preserving the essence of what makes a Bugatti—a harmonious blend of speed, beauty, and innovation—while evolving to meet the demands of a new generation of enthusiasts. With expanded facilities in Molsheim, a growing global presence, and a design philosophy that continues to marry form and function in breathtaking ways, Bugatti is not just building cars; it’s crafting experiences, curating dreams, and setting new standards for what luxury and performance can mean in the 21st century and beyond.

Records:

Bugatti has set numerous world records that showcase its dominance in speed, engineering, and performance. The EB110, launched in the early 1990s, achieved a top speed of 351 km/h in its Super Sport variant and became the fastest car powered by natural gas at 344.7 km/h, while also setting a record for the fastest production car on ice at 296.34 km/h. The Veyron 16.4, introduced in 2005, broke barriers with a top speed of 408 km/h, and its Super Sport version pushed that even further to 431 km/h, earning the title of the world’s fastest production car in 2010. The Grand Sport Vitesse later became the fastest roadster, reaching 408 km/h. Bugatti continued its record-breaking legacy with the Chiron, which stunned the world by accelerating from 0 to 400 km/h and back to 0 in just 41.96 seconds. In 2019, the Chiron Super Sport 300+ became the first production car to exceed 300 mph, clocking in at 304.773 mph (490.484 km/h), setting a new benchmark for hypercar performance. These records reflect Bugatti’s relentless pursuit of pushing the limits of automotive technology, combining extreme speed with luxury, safety, and precision engineering.
